WORLD CANON
  • Scope: this world's story is the Forgotten Realms' story — the Realms as they were written, carried faithfully up to the day the world ended, and from there bent by the APOC and by Shaun's rulings. No other world's history walks here. What Eberron and the rest can lend is tools — equipment, spells, items, feats, the shapes of classes — with their lore stripped away at the gate, the way the Bastion strips everything at its gates.

  • Timeline: the Realms' history runs true and unbroken — until the APOC. That is where the old canon ends, mid-sentence. Then a thousand years pass over the corpse of the world, and the story picks up in 2701 DR, in the ruin and the fog. Everything in those thousand years between is Shaun's lore, and it is still being written.
    Original wording

  • The APOC: it happened because heroes failed. The original party went up against the Demon God where he lay imprisoned in Limbo, and they lost. The god walked free, Laney was taken, and the Abyss tore open across the world. In a single day, eighty percent of all life was dead. The crystal sphere itself was scarred shut, and every road between the planes closed forever — no one has left this world since, and nothing has come to save it.
    Original wording

    The APOC: the original party failed against the Demon God imprisoned in Limbo — god freed, Laney captured, Abyssal rifts, 80% of life dead in 24 hours, crystal sphere scarred over, all planar travel permanently severed.

  • Fog of Life: after the end, the fog came — the Fog of Life, lying over the whole surface of the world, all the way out to the crystal sphere. It does not sink underground, and it cannot pass the Bastion's mythals. It is always grey; no sun and no stars have been seen through it since. It is not damp — it does not wet anything it touches. And it cannot be touched back: it does not part, swirl, or answer to hand, wind, or spell. It simply is, inert and untouchable, the weather of the world's long aftermath.
    Original wording

    Fog of Life: surface only, out to the crystal sphere; does NOT work underground; does NOT penetrate the Bastion's mythals; always grey, no sun, no stars; NOT damp; INERT and UNTOUCHABLE.

  • Magic now: magic came through the end changed, and dangerous in a new way. Raw magic blights the land around whoever casts it — the ground rots where power passes. So the cornerstone of the age is alchemy, patient and physical. The old shortcuts are gone: extra-dimensional magic is dead, and a Bag of Holding, a Banishment, a Misty Step, a Blink — all of them simply fail. Elemental magic limps unless it is fed through alchemical components or steampunk apparatus. Summoning does not work at all: nothing answers. In their place rose the age's own tools — firearms, counted among the simple weapons, there for any trained hand. Potions obey the new physics of things: a true magic-item potion strikes at once, while an alchemical draught spreads its work evenly over as many rounds as the spell's level, the cure line paying out its Nd8+N at 1d8+1 a round. Mind and magic see through each other — psionics and spellcraft read as one — but no working of the mind can carry anyone beyond the Material Plane, and the Astral Construct is gone from the world entirely.
    Original wording

    Magic now: raw magic blights/rots land around the caster; alchemy is the cornerstone; extra-dimensional magic is dead (Bag of Holding, Banishment, Misty Step, Blink fail); elemental magic hampered unless augmented by alchemical components/steampunk apparatus; summoning spells DON'T WORK; firearms are SIMPLE weapons; potion rule (magic-item potions immediate; alchemical potions spread over rounds = spell level, cure line Nd8+N at 1d8+1/round); psionics–magic transparency; psychoportation beyond the Material fails; Astral Construct out.

  • Fantasy Physics document: underneath everything sits the old physics of the Realms — the Weave itself, the Shadow Weave beside it, spellfire, mythals as fields of standing power — the Fantasy Physics of the world as it was before the end, adopted as the baseline of how things work. Over that baseline this age has written its overrides: the planes are severed, extra-dimensional magic is dead, and firearms work, for Gond's old ban did not survive into 2701. The blight that follows raw casting is the Weave's post-APOC state, and what the Bastion's mythal does is defined by Shaun's own ward list, not the old books.
    Original wording

Out in the badlands it is permanent and multi-way: Life itself — the good, the neutral, and life's own evils standing shoulder to shoulder — against the demons, against the Kingdom of the Undead; and the Warforged fight as a side of their own, beholden to none of them.

The Remnants of Life, what is left of everything that refused to fall. The Kingdom of the Undead, patient and expanding. And the Warforged — free, sentient, and second-class citizens of the world they walk, a faction still deciding what it is.

CITY CANON — THE BASTION OF LAST HOPE
  • The Bastion IS Waterdeep: everything Waterdeep was, the Bastion still carries in its bones. Waterdeep's Forgotten Realms history is the Bastion's own pre-APOC past — its streets, its wards, its Palace and Castle, Undermountain yawning below it — all of it happened here, to this city, before the world ended. The Ruins of Undermountain are core Bastion material, and Shaun's sourcebook #6 was built from the original City of Waterdeep book, because there was never any other foundation to build on: the city did not start over after the APOC. It refused to. What stands in 2701 DR is Waterdeep a thousand years after the end of the world, renamed by survival — Waterdeep is its original name, and the Bastion of Last Hope is the name it carries now, the name it earned by being one of the four places on the planet that did not fall.
  • The Plateau and the Wall: the city does not sit on the plain, and it never did. Undercliff is ground level — and the Bastion stands on a plateau a hundred feet above it, so the whole city looks down on the land at its feet the way it looks down on everything that comes at it. On that plateau rises the wall, a hundred feet of it, cannoneer towers along its length, so that from the ground at Undercliff to the top of the wall is two hundred feet straight up: a hundred feet of earth, a hundred feet of wall, and behind it a city that has made a doctrine of being hard to reach. Every approach to the Bastion is a climb, and every climb is a killing ground. The height is not scenery. The height is the first sentence of the city's defence, and everything else — the mythal, the towers, the constructs, the Guard — is written after it.
  • Defences: four layers, each answering a different kind of death. (1) The Melairshield mythal, the invisible dome of wards that decides what may exist inside the city at all. (2) The cannoneer-towered wall on its hundred-foot plateau, for everything that comes on foot or by siege. (3) The walking gargantuan constructs, the city's moving fortresses, slow answers to enormous problems. (4) The city guard — and here the city makes its coldest distinction: the Guard looks OUTWARD, toward the badlands and the war, while the city watch looks INWARD, Judge Dredd-like, toward the citizens. The split exists because the city is packed — life boiling over inside the walls, a whole population pressed into towers — and a packed city is a danger to itself that has to be policed as seriously as any army outside.
  • Mythal wards — the Melairshield: the mythal is the reason the Bastion is the Bastion. It keeps OUT aerial and flying creatures, demons, undead, and dragons, and the Fog of Life does not penetrate it — inside the ward, the air is the city's own, the grey held at the boundary like weather against a window. But the mythal is not a perfect dome with a hard edge. Past the walls, on the lower southern side, it spreads thin and weak and flutters like a candle — and that guttering edge shapes whole districts of life, from Shantytown huddled in its half-light to Low Man's Hole lying beyond it altogether. Everything the mythal bars is a thing the city has already survived once; the ward list is a memorial as much as a defence, and the day of the dracolich proved why every name on it is there.
  • The Unwriting (about 300 years into the apocalypse): a vast undead army came at the Bastion out of the east, demons at its head, and at its spearpoint the one creature that was everything the Melairshield was raised to keep out, all of it in a single body — a flying dracolich: dragon and undead and demon-led horror in one. It came down on the very top of Mount Waterdeep and there cast an epic-level working that did not destroy, did not banish, but UNWROTE: a perfect sphere centered on the peak, hundreds of feet across, everything within it struck out of the annals of existence — not teleported away, not disintegrated, unwritten, so thoroughly unwritten that the oldest elf in the city will tell you it has always been like that, as far as he knew. The sphere's reach took the eastern edge of Mount Waterdeep and ran west all the way down into the water, and the perfect scoop of it is there to be seen in the ground below the surface. Yet Mount Waterdeep still stands — a portion of it remains — so history does not read the mountain as gone. The sea came through the wound all at once, rushing into every open channel under the city, the sewers and Undermountain itself, until the pressure stood manholes on columns of water sixty, seventy feet in the air. The city flooded — and the city held. It beat back the flood and the army both, repaired itself, and bolstered the circle that was left behind. The Unwriting is the city's second founding: everything built in the circle since — the docks, the Heart — stands on the ground where the dracolich tried to erase the Bastion from ever having been.
  • The Heart of the City: inside the unwritten sphere the Bastion built something no other age would have dared: megastructure industrial floating docks, raised on the water within the circle. Because of the mythal, engineering and architecture can stand on those docks that the tide should tear apart — the docks ride the water, floating some fifty feet up and fifty feet down with the tide, and the buildings on them, towers the size of the Empire State Building, do not topple. The district rises and falls, rises and falls, patient as breathing. It is called the Heart of the City because it beats like a heart — and the name is more than poetry: the docks are where the city's heavy industry lives, where the consortiums' great works are floated and fitted, an industrial heart pumping ships, machines, and materiel out into a city at permanent war. The cross wall runs to meet the docks, so the Heart is tied into the city's defences as well as its economy; the place the dracolich unwrote has become the place the city cannot live without.
  • Megastructures are Class A: the city's giants — fifty, sixty stories, each on a footprint the size of the Empire State Building's — stand in the first class of the city's buildings, ranked with the Palace and the Castle themselves; the landmarks of the old city have become the towers of the new. They stand on the floating docks of the Heart, and they stand in the city proper, planted among the older streets, breaking the old grid around their enormous bases. And whatever any map shows, the map lies by understatement: there are more of them on the ground than are drawn. Between them runs the city's second sky — soft walkways and hard walkways, overpasses and overbridges, crossing and recrossing at every height, until in places a man looks up from the street and there is no sky left to see, only bridges stacked on bridges over his head. The megastructures are why a citizen can live a whole life in one building, why status is measured in floors, why the watch polices a vertical city of stacked lives — they are not buildings the city has. They are the shape the city's survival took.
  • Society: LAWFUL EVIL with neutral tendencies, ruled by an institution whose iron thumb is on everything — and the city does not like change, which is precisely why it held on when the world fell. A city that refuses to change does not panic, does not scatter, does not reinvent itself mid-catastrophe; it grips what it has and endures. Its people live vertically, in Mega-City One-like mega-structures, stacked by status and by contract: Little Timmy is born on the 32nd floor, his grandfather lives on the 40th, his father on the 2nd, his school is on the 12th, his market on the 10th, his park is on the roof — and he may never leave the building in his life. That is not a curiosity. That is the ordinary Bastion childhood, multiplied by towers: a whole life, birth to burial, inside one address, in a city where the streets belong to the watch and the sky belongs to the bridges.
  • Status geography: in the Bastion, worth has coordinates, and everyone can read them. Status runs north and up: the top floors of the northern buildings are high society, the city's crown; lower and further south is lesser, floor by floor and street by street, until the first floor of a southern building is almost as bad as outside the wall itself. An address is a biography — tell a Bastionite your building and your floor and they know your family's standing, your consortium's weight, and your prospects, before you give your name. The geography is the society: the plateau lifts the whole city above the world, the wall lifts it above the badlands, the towers lift the rich above the poor, and at the very bottom of every gradient, outside and below, sits Shantytown in the guttering mythal-light, looking up.
  • Shantytown: outside the wall, on the lower, southern side — the bottom of every gradient the city has. There the mythal spreads past the walls weak and fluttering like a candle, and outlanders settle in that half-protection for what it gives, because a flickering ward is still more than the badlands offer. The place is built from the city's REFUSE plus badlands finds, and the loop never stops: the municipal dump, north-northeast of the farms, feeds it — a good share of what the city throws away ends up taken by Shantytown and built into the place the city pretends not to see. Shantytown is the Bastion's shadow and its antechamber: no one enters the city without business or work, so everyone who arrives with neither washes up here, in the candlelight, under the wall, waiting for a contract to lift them inside — or dying within sight of the gates.
  • Entry rule: no entry without business there or work for somebody — no tourists, no drifters, no lollygagging. The Bastion is not a refuge; it is an employer and a market, and its gates are a hiring hall and a customs house, not a sanctuary door. Entry itself is a civil status, held under somebody's contract: you are in the city because a guild, a school, a business, or a consortium has paper on you, and when the paper ends, so does your welcome. This is why the Guard watches the gates for access and the Watch observes every entrant — a man inside the walls without a contract is not a visitor. He is a discrepancy, and the city corrects discrepancies.
  • Economy: guilds, schools, businesses, and the 24 consortiums, each of many businesses — and the first law of the Bastion's economy is that they SELL to each other; they don't give. Everything runs on contracts, and ALL contracts are temporary, because a permanent obligation would be a power the city does not allow anyone to hold. The consortiums build what each other needs to bolster their individual armies — for the war outside the walls is permanent, and every consortium fields its own strength — and when a contract dispute cannot be settled at a table, it is settled in the badlands, by the armies the contracts built. Money, law, and war are one machine in the Bastion: the contract is the city's true code, the badlands are its courtroom of last resort, and the whole apparatus is why a packed, vertical, lawful-evil city can feed itself, arm itself, and hold a wall for a thousand years.
  • Reclamation Item Retrieval: ONE job, ONE business — among the many big businesses of the city, and the one that explains adventurers. Reclamation Item Retrieval is what brings characters out of the Bastion and into the badlands: a thousand years of the dead world's treasures lie buried out there in the decimation, and the consortiums pay to have them dug up, hauled back, and sold under contract. It is salvage as an industry — the badlands mined for the pre-APOC world, one expedition at a time — and it sits at the exact crossing point of the city's great systems: contracts fund it, armies escort it, the badlands settle its disputes, and its finds feed the markets inside the walls. Saint James is NOT part of it and has nothing to do with the city — he is an independent operator in the badlands, and the distinction matters: Reclamation is the city's hand reaching out; Saint James is what the badlands grew on their own.
  • Protan Consortium: the founding example of what a consortium is. The Protan Consortium's main business is battle wagons — great armored wagons built to stand up to the badlands and to the Hatestorms themselves — sold to whoever can pay. In a world where the weather can kill an army and every contract dispute is settled outdoors, the wagon that survives the road is worth more than the cargo it carries; Protan sells survival, mounted and rolling, and every other consortium is its customer, its rival, or both.
  • The City of the Dead: the necropolis stands on the same level as the living city — its dead are not lowered away or set beneath, but walled beside their descendants at the city's own height, and it has its own wall around it, a city within the city, quiet on the plateau. In the Bastion, death is not the opposite of citizenship; the dead keep their address. Generations of a family stack in the necropolis the way they stacked in the towers, floor above floor, and the living go through its gates to tend what is theirs — which is why the ground beyond its wall, where the mythal fails, is such a precise cruelty by contrast.
  • Low Man's Hole: to the right of the City of the Dead lies a circle in the earth — Low Man's Hole, a hundred feet deep measured from Undercliff, which puts its floor three hundred feet below the top of the wall and two hundred below the streets of the city. An odd slope runs down to it, through very old gothic gates, in a spooky, falling-down kind of scenario. Low Man's Hole is not kept by the city. It is not maintained. It lies outside the mythal, in the flickering edge of its protection, where the ward-light gutters like a candle — the same candlelight that shelters Shantytown, but fallen further, past the wall's regard entirely — and sometimes Grandpa gets restless, and wakes up from the dead, and climbs. Then the towers on the wall open up and blast the undead into pieces where they stand: the wall's guns are the only municipal service Low Man's Hole has ever received. No one comes down to clean it up afterward. Broken skeletal parts lie exposed where they fell, and have lain there for decades. Family members occasionally come down and tidy what is theirs — the same families who tend their dead in honor inside the City of the Dead, picking through bone in the dark outside it — for the most part, the place is simply left to lack, and to rot, and to wait.
  • The Cross Wall and the Double Doors: above Low Man's Hole runs a wall, left to right, tying into the wall of the City of the Dead on one hand and running on to connect with the docks of the Heart on the other — one seam of stone stitching the necropolis, the harbor of floating towers, and the pit of the unburied into the city's single defensive line. Two roads pass through that wall, and where each road passes there stand very large double doors, set over the roadway to disconnect it. The doors generally always stay open — the roads breathe through them day and night, traffic flowing between the dead, the docks, and the city — but in times of emergency, or under duress, they close, and the road is a road no longer. They are the city's fingers on that seam: open in trust, shut in a fist the moment the city decides the east side of its own wall has become the outside.
  • The Prison and Arkham: at the top of the city rises a large hill, and on top of that hill, outside the city gates, sits the city prison, on the surface, in the open grey. Below it — under the hill, under the prison — lies the city mental institution. Its name is Arkham Asylum. The placement is the city's philosophy in one hill: punishment where everyone can see it, above the gates, a warning with an address; madness where no one has to. A man can be sent up the hill to be locked away, or down it to be forgotten — and in a lawful-evil city under an iron thumb, the distance between those two sentences is shorter than anyone inside admits.
  • The Ziggaport: the one place in all the Bastion where flying vessels may legally dock. It stands above the city to the north, off to the right of the prison hill — the city's punishment below it, the city's sky-gate beside it, the two of them keeping the north together. The mythal bars flying creatures from the city's sky, but hulls and rigging are another matter — trade comes out of the grey on wings whether the city wills it or not, so the city did what it does with everything: it gave the sky a single gate, and put the gate under law. Everywhere else above the towers is closed; what arrives on wings arrives at the Ziggaport, or it does not arrive at all.
  • The Municipal Farms and the Dump: off to the right of the city stand the city municipal farms — farms above ground and farms below ground, and between them they make all the food for the city. A walled city at permanent war cannot eat from the badlands; the farms are as much a fortification as the wall, a breadbasket under guard, feeding towers that hold more people than the land inside the walls could ever grow for. The kitchens are inside the city — the farms only grow. North-northeast of the farms lies the city dump, the end of the city's other pipeline: everything the towers consume comes down at last to the dump, and from the dump, by the unofficial economy no contract governs, out to Shantytown, which builds itself from the leavings. Farms feed the city; the city feeds the dump; the dump feeds the shadow outside the wall. Nothing in the Bastion is wasted, not even its refuse — it is just re-sold, re-built, and re-inhabited, one gradient down.
  • The Laws (locked in by Shaun, 2026-11-06): in the Bastion, everything is a written contract, every contract is temporary, and breaking a contract is the core crime from which all other crimes descend. At the gates the duty splits: the Guard controls access, while the Watch observes everyone who enters, pursues fugitives, and escorts visitors through a city they are only visiting. The Watch serves under Captain Rulathon, who reports to the Open Lords; every ward keeps its civilars, its guard posts, its skulk and its arcanum, all of it based at the Palace, and above every court sits the Lord's Court, whose word is final. The law is a printed thing: rulings and decrees come off the city's restricted presses — Gilder's — and no other press may print them. What the law tolerates, it tolerates for commerce's sake: faiths and festivals go on freely so long as they break no law and do not interfere with the flow of trade. And when nothing else remains, the Gray Hands answer — directly to the Open Lords, and only then. The Walking Statues stir against any creature of Huge size or greater caught in destructive, hostile action against the city, and defend themselves when seriously attacked. Inside the administration itself, the South Brigade Precinct hunts crime and corruption in the city's own ranks. The civil side of the code runs on the same contract logic: breaking terms, letting a debt run past its term, fraud, selling outside your contract, blocking commerce — these are answered with fines, restitution, or labor under contract. Crimes against the city itself cut deeper: raw magic; attempted summoning or extra-dimensional working; violence in the towers; theft from the city stores, the wall, or the war effort; forgery of a contract, a decree, or a gate pass; smuggling in, or harboring, anything the mythal bars; private war inside the walls; treason. Those are answered with imprisonment, expulsion beyond the wall, or death.
    Original wording

    The Laws (LOCKED CANON 2026-11-06): everything is a written, temporary contract; breaking a contract is the core crime; gates split Guard (access) / Watch (observes entrants, pursues fugitives, escorts visitors); Watch under Captain Rulathon reporting to the Open Lords (ward civilars, guard posts, a skulk, an arcanum per ward; base: the Palace); Lord's Court final; rulings/decrees printed on the city's restricted presses (Gilder's); faiths/festivals tolerated so long as they violate no law and don't interfere with commerce; Gray Hands answer directly to the Open Lords, called only when nothing else remains; Walking Statues act against any Huge+ creature engaged in destructive/hostile activity against the city and defend themselves against serious attack; South Brigade Precinct fights crime and corruption inside the administration.   • Raw magic scope: forbidden in principle EVERYWHERE; enforceable only inside the Bastion (no Watch in the badlands). Inside the walls, raw magic is not merely a crime but a blasphemy against survival itself — the city remembers what uncontrolled power did to the world — while beyond the wall the principle stands unenforced, one more law the badlands ignore.
  • Slavery & Warforged: abolition is RECENT, and it happened because of the Warforged. They were created, not born — built as labor and as soldiers, "just toasters" in the phrase of everyone who owned one — until they gained sentience and the city had to decide what a person is. It is about 1965 for the Warforged: a long couple of decades of 1965, unfinished — free on paper, lesser in practice, second-class citizens in the towers they may well have built. The in-world slur for them is "toi" (T-O-I), and it carries the full weight such words carry; the world's bigotry is represented in the material as it is, not sanitized. The Warforged thread runs through everything else in the city: they are why slavery ended, they are part of why the consortiums' armies look as they do, and their unfinished freedom is the loudest proof that the city which does not like change can still be changed — slowly, and at cost.
LAWS OF THE BASTION

Part 1: Foundations. Canon — locked in by Shaun on 2026-11-06. Built from Document #6's own scattered law content and Shaun's confirmed 2701 canon.

The Bastion has no single law book, and it does not need one. Law in the Bastion is not a code you consult; it is the shape of the city itself — the thumb on every street, the name on every contract, the watchman on the corner who is also the judge. A citizen does not ask what the law says. A citizen asks what the watch will say, and already knows the answer closely enough to behave.

WHO MAKES THE LAW

At the top sit the Open Lords, and law comes down from them the way decrees come off Gilder's press — the press is restricted to the Bastion's own use, and the Councils print their rulings and distribute them through the city. A law is a physical thing you can hold: a printed decree, posted and handed out, its text owned by the city that made it. Nobody else prints the law.

WHO CARRIES IT

Two forces, two directions. The city guard faces outward — the wall, the towers, the gates — and the city watch faces inward; the watch is the law a citizen actually lives under. Under Commander Captain Rulathon, who reports to the Open Lords, the watch is the Bastion's police: every ward has its civilars, its guard posts, its skulk and its arcanum, and the base of it all is the Palace. In a city packed this tight, life boiling over in every mega-structure, the watch carries a Dredd-like weight: judgment and enforcement arrive in the same person, on the same street corner.

THE GATES

Entry is the first law every outsider meets. The guard controls access and the watch observes those who enter, pursues fugitives, and escorts visitors in — because nobody enters the Bastion without business there or work for somebody. No tourists, no drifters; you are in the Bastion because the Bastion has a use for you, on a contract or an errand, and when the business ends, so does the welcome.

CONTRACTS ARE THE CITY'S TRUE CODE

Everything runs on contracts — an apple cart's pitch, a guildhall's charter, the rights to a mining town out in the wastes — and every contract is temporary. Law between citizens, guilds, and consortiums is contract law: they sell to each other, they do not give; they build what each other needs to bolster their individual armies; and when a contract dispute cannot be settled inside the walls, it is settled out in the badlands. The watch enforces contracts inside the city. Outside, the parties enforce their own.

THE ONE ABSOLUTE

Raw magic is absolutely forbidden within the walls. Not licensed, not taxed, not tolerated in small amounts — forbidden. Raw magic blights; a city of mega-structures, floor upon floor of life stacked forty stories deep, cannot afford blight in its foundations. What the mythal keeps out at the border of the sky, the law keeps out at the level of the street.

THE STANDING TOLERANCES

What the law does not forbid, it mostly ignores — so long as the flow of commerce is not interfered with. Every faith may keep its festivals, every guild its holy days and processions, so long as they do not violate the law or interfere with the flow of commerce; the Lord's Court itself closes on Ahghairon's Day. And inside the watch itself, bodies like the South Brigade Precinct fight the criminal element and corruption in the watch's own ranks.

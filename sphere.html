Since the APOC — the Closing, called the Cut

A thousand years after the apocalypse, the placements above stand exactly as the record sets them. What changed is not where things are — it is what can reach them.

Scarred shut

The sphere is scarred shut. Nothing crosses the wall in either direction, and all planar travel is severed — permanently.

The gods are outside

The gods are beyond the wall. They can see in, but they cannot touch, interact with, or influence anything inside the sphere.

The Fog of Life

From Toril's surface, neither sun nor stars are seen. The Fog of Life fills the sky — grey light and grey dark. The Sun still burns at the center; it is simply never seen.

Power is drawn, not granted

The gods' silence changes nothing about the placements. Inside the sphere, power is drawn, not granted — every world sits where the record put it.

The other worlds

In and of themselves, the other planets have not directly been touched by portals — no rifts opened on them. But incursions have been made by the demons, with Toril as the demons' home base for those incursions.

Stations of the sphere

The Rock of Bral — spelled B-R-A-L, pronounced “brawl” — still floats anchored above the Bastion, out in deep space off Toril's surface — a trading point between the cities. A couple of other stations exist besides it; their names and placements are not in the record.

The Rock of Bral — the station, updated

A city that hangs over a city

From the Bastion's streets it cannot be seen — the Fog of Life lies grey and unbroken over the world, no sun, no stars — yet a whole other city hangs above that ceiling in black silence and perfect starlight. Ships climb up out of the fog and break into the black, and the Rock swings into view: a mountain of stone in the void, terraced with docks and lantern-lit streets. As the old folk say below: the Bastion has a roof, and there's a market on the roof. It is anchored — held on station by anchor-keeper families who have kept that charge for generations, Rock-born who have never stood on Toril.

The trading point of the held world

The one place none of the cities owns, so the one table everybody can sit at: Bastion money, Silverymoon grace, Sundabar iron, and stranger coin from the far lanes. Through it flow Badlands salvage and reclamation treasure, alchemical work, steamcraft and apparatus, ship's stores beyond price — air, water, spars, helm-time, repairs — and news, which in a sealed sphere is cargo too. A trading point that cheats is a rock; a trading point that keeps faith is a city in the sky.

The name, and the brawling

Say Bral in a dockside tavern and it comes out brawl — and the Rock lives up to its pronunciation: the whole surviving world in one stone bottle, armed, proud, and certain the last crew cheated them, tipping periodically into a glorious dock-wide brawl and drinking together an hour later, because the man thrown through the table is the only engineer in port who can fix your helm. Two rules are iron for everybody: no weapons out, and nobody gets thrown off.

The sealed harbour

In the old days this would have been a door — ships burning in from the Phlogiston out of other spheres. That door is shut, and everybody on the Rock knows precisely how shut. It is a harbour on a sea that now ends at a wall — and yet it still floats: empires fell, the sky below went grey forever, and the station held its anchor, kept its lanterns lit, and went on trading. They're looking, we're trading — the most Rock sentence ever spoken.

The steampunk treatment — the same stone, kept alive by apparatus

The Rock underneath stays the Rock — same stone, same anchor, same docks, same spelljamming workings underneath and identifiable. What a thousand years of the APOC age laid over it is steam, because up there the law of the age is a founding engineering fact: a station lives or dies by workings that cannot be allowed to flicker — its air, its anchor, its heat, its light — so the Rock long ago stopped trusting bare magic with its life and built machines around every working that matters.

  • The Anchor Engine. Where the anchor-wards once stood alone, a cathedral of machinery bored through the heart of the rock: Dwarven Forges burning in a row like captured suns, married to flywheels, piston-towers, and miles of riveted pipe, holding the Rock on station with a slow heartbeat old hands feel in the soles of their boots. The anchor-keeper families keep their ancient charge as engineer-priests of the Engine. When the Engine coughs, the whole Rock goes quiet. It does not cough often.
  • Iron lungs — the air works. Vast alchemical-steam airworks churn day and night in the deep levels — condensers, scrubbers, pressure halls with riveted cathedral doors, forests of copper pipe. Air-gardens grow green under banks of mirror-lamps, and the boundary between garden and engine room has dissolved: vines on the boilers, boilers among the vines. A captain buying air on the Rock buys pressure, by the gauge, certified by the Airworks stamp — trusted in every port the sealed sphere has left.
  • The docks, rebuilt in iron. The wooden gantries are long gone: steam cranes walking their rails on the rim, articulated loader-arms, pressurized docking collars, cannon foundries serving the same interchangeable steamcraft pool the ground war uses. A ship can be re-armed, re-boilered, re-rigged, and re-provisioned on the same terrace. Ballista and bombard sit in steam-traversed mountings, and black powder is milled and misfire-tested under Rock safety law — the one set of rules even brawling captains do not argue with.
  • Heat, light, and the look of it. The Rock glows amber: pressure-lamp and alchemical burner, black iron, brass gone green-brown at the edges, copper pipe, rivets in regiments, glass gauges like a thousand small watchful eyes, steam venting in white plumes off the rim terraces and freezing into the black in slow, glittering fans — the station breathing out. Inside: steam organs announcing arrivals, pneumatic message-tubes, clockwork tally-boards flipping prices, lifts on chains and counterweights whose operators call the levels in a chant older than their machines.
  • The people, retooled. The War-Torn walk its terraces in numbers out of all proportion to the ground, fitted and maintained by Rock prosthetic shops drawing on the same shared pool as Warforged components and steamcraft armor. Warforged work the Engine levels and the heavy docks; Giff officers have gone happily half-mechanical in appointment and tailoring; Hadozee riggers run the crane-rails. Even the brawls have a house rule the old Rock never needed: iron stays down — piston arms and forge-hands checked at the tavern door like weapons.
  • What steam did not change. Ships still move by helm and Ship's Rating, air is still an envelope, gravity still lies in planes, the sphere is still scarred shut over it all, and the gods still watch without touching. The steampunk Rock is not a different station — it is the same ancient trading stone, kept alive into a new age the only way anything in the APOC world stays alive: by alchemy, by apparatus, and by engineers who refuse to let it die.

The peoples of the Rock walk its concourses as ordinary citizens of the void — Dracon, Giff, Hadozee, Scro, Hurwaet, Rastipede, and Xixchil, the Arcane passing through wherever helms are dealt in — alongside Bastion, Silverymoon, and Sundabar folk and the Rock-born themselves, quietly convinced the entire population of Toril lives upside-down at the bottom of a gravity well out of sheer stubbornness. A couple of other stations exist besides the Rock; their names and placements are not in the record.

The Spelljammer layer — canon in this sphere

Spelljammer is Shaun's standing exception, and it stands here in full: everything he has brought into the game — the ships and their deck plans, the helms, the peoples of space, the spells, the gear, and how it all works — is canon for this sphere and rides on this map. The APOC state still governs it all: the wall stays scarred shut, there is no planar travel, and there is no summoning. What sails, sails inside the sphere.

How it works

A ship flies because a helm drinks a spellcaster's power and turns it into motion. Around every hull rides an envelope of air the crew breathes; gravity settles along a plane through the ship, so decks hold "down" even in the void. Beyond the sphere wall lies the Phlogiston, the rainbow flow between spheres — sailed for ages before the Cut, and cut off now with everything else outside.

Under the Cut

The workings themselves never changed — helms still answer, air still holds, ships still ply wildspace between the worlds on the orrery above. What the Closing took is the way out: the old portal craft is kept in the record as written, but nothing crosses the scarred wall, in either direction, ever.

The Spelljammer — the named ship, updated & steampunkified

This is the particular named ship — not the crystal sphere, and not spelljamming in general. Her. The great living ship, three-quarters of a mile across, city-crowned, masterless, that no fleet owns and no helm but her own can move. Her two maps ride here with her entry, and every point of her record — published, APOC, and steampunk — is elaborated below.

The published record — the points, as printed

  • She is an enigma of space. Obviously a constructed thing — built, by somebody, for something — yet her great size should forbid the speed and maneuverability every tale credits her with.
  • There is apparently only one. She has been reported destroyed on several occasions, only to reappear again later, like a phoenix rising from its ashes.
  • She has no master. Said to have been commanded by men, elves, illithids, and beholders — yet usually, when encountered, she has no master and no central control, but speeds along aimlessly on her own course.
  • She is the size of a small city. Her surface is dotted with huge castles and citadels; her interior is hollow, laced with huge decks, storage areas, even internal greenhouses under magical illumination.
  • A continual war burns aboard her between the factions in her various parts — at least three beholder nations, a mind flayer outpost, several pirate ships, a small herd of umber hulks escaped from the neogi, and stranger creatures still — small raids against the other sides, while each faction searches for its ultimate goal.
  • The ultimate goal is the helm — the helm which controls the ship. Standard helms have no effect on her; she is even immune to the Crown of the Stars — though factions aboard say a similar artifact, this “ultimate helm,” controls her, and it may have a versatile and changing form, which could explain her differing appearance in different tales.
  • The gods themselves are silent when it comes to her. Commune and contact higher plane produce negligible or misleading information about her — sages suggest, quietly, that she may be outside the limit of the gods' knowledge. Such spells work normally aboard her, when the subject is anything else.
  • She is an erratic beast — unseen in the Known Spheres for generations, then making a rapid series of appearances throughout the spheres; in some worlds tied to dark times and coming tragedy, in others a symbol of the gods' involvement, in still others merely a pretty astronomical occurrence.
  • Her listed weapons are only the ones known to be aboard. Stories tell of great armories deep within her hull, protected by magical guardians, holding all manner of additional equipment including the rumored bombards. Anyone approaching to land may find themselves the target of a group effort by the established factions, who want no further competition for the helm.
  • The questions stand unanswered on the poster itself: Where did she come from? Who built her? And for what purpose? How does she move through space like some huge radiant dragon? Who steers her now?

First sight of her

Nothing in wildspace prepares you for the proportions. From above she is all wing — two vast purple sweeps curving forward into pale horn-tips, the city packed along her spine like a town that grew on a bridge. From the side, stranger and better: the wing sweeps back into a curling tail, and the city rides her back in full skyline, spires and battlements and a great gate-arch, like a capital that has gone to sea on the back of some patient, enormous beast. Her head is a beak. Her tail is a rudder and a sting. Sailors who have seen her once spend the rest of their lives as the people who have seen her once.

Nobody's ship, everybody's war

Understand the war, because aboard her the war is the government. There is a Captain's Tower, a Captain's Quarters, a Council — and that is not the same as a captain. You can hold a tower, a wing, a garden, a battery. You cannot hold her. So the conquerors settle: fortify their tower, marry their neighbours' cousins, raid the next district on feast days, and become one more faction in the continual war — every one of them still searching, every one certain the goal is one corridor closer than last year. She sails on through all of it, aimless, masterless, utterly unbothered: the only vessel whose crew is a civilization and whose mutiny never ends because it never quite began.

Inside a sealed sphere — the APOC update

  • Nowhere else to go. Her record was written for a universe with open doors — unseen for generations, then a rapid series of appearances throughout the spheres. Inside a sealed sphere that rhythm turns colder: the sphere she vanishes in is finite and walled, and everyone in it knows that wherever she is when she is unseen, she is still in here, behind the same scarred sky. Sailors of the age do not say she has gone. They say she has gone quiet — and when the sightings begin again, port after port, the whole sealed world argues about what it means in every tavern, exactly as her record says the worlds always did.
  • The gods' silence, doubled. The poster said the gods grow strangely quiet when the questions turn to her. In this sphere the gods are trapped outside the wall — they can see in, and they cannot touch, interact with, or influence anything within. So the old silence now has the shape of the age itself: divinations aimed at the world work; divinations aimed at her come back thin, misleading, or drowned in the general silence of a heaven locked outdoors. The factions aboard received the Closing with a shrug: of course the heavens went quiet. They went quiet here first.
  • The war aboard, seasoned. A thousand years have not ended it — but the cut roads changed its stakes: in the sealed sphere there is no elsewhere worth the name, and her factions were always lifers. Her decks are not exempt from the age — demon incursions trouble the worlds below, and she is a city, and cities in this age hold or fall — but she is the hardest target in the sphere to besiege: a fortress that moves like a dragon, answers to no helm an enemy can seize, and repels boarders with the rare, united fury of all her factions at once.
  • Reported destroyed. The tales still come in across the thousand years — the great ship broken over a dead moon, burned in a cosmic storm, gone down into a sun — and then, a generation later, a silhouette three-quarters of a mile across, city-crowned, sliding unhurried through the stars. Whether she truly dies and rises, or whether a city that size simply cannot be confirmed killed by anyone not standing on her when it happened, is one more of her unanswered questions.
  • Her course, now. Aimless — and inside a finite sphere, an aimless course is a slow patrol of everything there is: the lanes between the held worlds, the long dark past Glyth and Garden, the approaches to the Rock of Bral, where her passing empties the Rock's concourses as every soul crowds the rim terraces to watch the legend cross the starfield like a moon with towers. If she is searching, the sphere is small enough that she has searched all of it. She searches it again.

The steampunk treatment — the living ship under a skin of steam

The Spelljammer underneath stays the Spelljammer: the same living manta-city, the same hollow hull, the same factions, the same ultimate helm no standard helm can touch — all of it kept underneath and identifiable. What a thousand years of the age laid over her is the same thing it laid over the Bastion and the Rock of Bral, scaled to a small city that flies: steam. Aboard her it is a civilization's founding problem — her greenhouses, her water, her heat, her light, the very pressure of her air were magic's work, and magic in this age flickers unless machinery cradles it. So her peoples built the apparatus into her.

  • The hollow engine decks. Deep below the storage cathedrals and greenhouse light stand the great boiler levels — furnaces married to alchemical fireboxes, dwarven Forge-engines sweated alongside gnomish contraptions of magnificent optimism, piston halls whose slow stroke is felt in the deck plates above like a second, iron heartbeat. No district owns the boilers, because no district can be allowed to stop them; the engine levels are the closest thing aboard to neutral ground — a truce enforced by thermodynamics. Engineers are her one true aristocracy of function: a master of the boiler levels can walk through a raid with his tools on his shoulder and be waved through by both sides, because both sides breathe his air.
  • Iron lungs, at city scale. Alchemical scrubber galleries run the length of the hollow hull; condenser forests sweat in the cold between decks; pressure halls with riveted doors the size of gates sigh day and night. The greenhouses now grow under banks of apparatus lamps and mirror arrays, tended by gardener-engineers who read a leaf and a gauge with the same eye. A failure in the gardens is a failure in the lungs; everybody aboard knows the smell of the air the way sailors know weather.
  • The wings, retooled. Her vast purple wings remain living sail and sovereign territory — but the age has veined them with iron: steam-traversed mountings under the great guns, armored service ways along the wing spars like railway cuttings, crane rails and loader gantries at the wing roots, semaphore towers and pressure-lamp signal masts stitching the districts together across three-quarters of a mile of living deck.
  • The batteries, refitted. The ancient broadside is not replaced but served: steam winches and counterweight rams working the great catapult arms, iron-shot foundries and powder mills in the deep hull, traversing gear that swings a whole battery's arc on the word of one officer in a brass-railed pulpit with a speaking tube. The four faction batteries each wear the refit in their own people's style — a point of honor defended as fiercely as the guns. The bombards remain rumored, exactly as the poster left them.
  • The people, retooled. The War-Torn, fitted and rebuilt and maintained — her prosthetic shops draw on the one shared pool that serves Warforged, War-Torn, and steamcraft armor everywhere, and her fitters are a guild of the first rank. Warforged serve in her engine levels, her batteries, and her councils. Giff officers have taken to steam appointment and steam tailoring with the joy of a people who always believed war should be dressed for. And one house rule of the age has hardened into custom aboard her, straight off the Rock's dockside law: iron stays down — when the war flashes into a brawl, piston arms and forge-hands are checked at the door beside the blades. The raids are war, and war is honest. The brawls are family, and family has rules.
  • What steam did not change. None of it moves her. Boilers do not move her. Airworks do not move her. She moves the way she always moved — by the ultimate helm, whatever and wherever it is. The steam is how her city survives the age. The mystery is how her ship ignores it. Both of those are her, entire, at the same time.

The forty-one points — every numbered area of the deck plans, elaborated

Each area stands under its own number, as the plans key it — the published place underneath, the APOC update and the steam laid over it. Areas 4 and 5 sub-key the glassteel window, council stage, auditorium, balcony, upper patio, personal quarters, storage, and common room; Area 6, the open-air public house, food stalls, jewelry, dwarvish weapons and art, crafts, and the upper warehouse above; Areas 9 through 14, the halfling entrance and family quarters, mess areas, tower armories and storage, the Plaza of Humanity, Grand Hall, audience halls, studies, and day rooms.

1) Landing Deck

The front door of a city with no ground beneath it — a field broad enough to take a squadron, steam cranes and gantries swinging out, and the deck crews, the oldest neutral institution aboard, moving among arrivals with the power of people who control the only door. Everything and everyone aboard came through here once. The crews keep the lamps lit anyway; she has been reported destroyed before. Ships still come.

2) Gardens

A wing of green across the back of a manta in the void — terraced growth on the living wing, tended by the oldest continuous profession aboard. The Gardens are her table and her lungs' first chamber at once; apparatus lamps and mirror rigs now stand among the orchard rows like a second, iron crop. Every faction eats from the Gardens, and by the ship's oldest custom, armies that raid each other by tower have never once raided a harvest.

3) Smalljammer Strips

The long landing strips ruled along her forward deck for the smalljammers — the little living ships, her own kind in miniature, the closest thing she has to a recognized embassy craft. Whole diplomacy happens here under truce-flag. In the sealed age the strips matter more, not less: smalljammer traffic between the great ship and the worlds below is one of the few threads still stitching her wandering city into the life of the sphere.

4) Council Chambers

Council stage, auditorium, balcony, and a great glassteel window opening on starfield and wing — a room built for the whole ship to be argued about in. Here the factions perform the ancient theatre of almost governing her; compacts sworn here hold longer than outsiders expect, because everyone in that auditorium knows the alternative is the war without the talking first. The steam age has added tally-boards, speaking tubes, and pneumatic lifts — and lost nothing of the true machinery, which is talk.

5) Captain's Quarters

Upper patio, personal quarters, storage, a common room — belonging, by the plan's plain naming, to the Captain. There is no captain, and the quarters are kept: swept, lit, provisioned, and waited in, by a small hereditary staff of stewards who serve an empty chair. To seize the quarters would prove nothing — a dozen captains in the tales had them, and the ship sailed on without consulting them. The rooms are a held breath: the ship's own admission that she was built to be commanded by someone.

6) Ship's Stores and Market

The commercial heart of the dorsal city, drawn down to the stall: open-air public house, food stalls, jewelry, dwarvish weapons and art, crafts and sundries, charms — and the long upper warehouse above. Every faction trades here under the market's own law, and prices are the ship's true news service: a captain fresh aboard can read the state of every war on her decks in the cost of grain, powder, and passage off. In the sealed age the Market has added sphere-goods — Bastion alchemy and Rock-minted coin — priced like relics from a smaller, louder world.

7) Captain's Tower

The tallest statement in her skyline — kitchens, meeting halls, quarters above quarters — and the twin of the Quarters' mystery. Expeditions have taken this tower more often than any other ground on the ship, and holding it has made exactly none of them her master. Its present holders enjoy the finest view in the known universe and the hollow prestige of a flag over a ship that does not answer flags. The tower is kept in full repair, steam-heated, lamp-lit, and garrisoned anyway. That, too, is life on the Spelljammer.

8) Library Tower

A tower given over entire to records — the one treasury every faction agrees is real: star-maps of spheres her peoples came from and will never see again, logs of her sightings across the centuries (each contradicting the last, filed volume by volume by librarians too honest to reconcile them), treaty rolls, genealogies. In the APOC age it has become an ark of the open universe — young sailors born in the jar come here to read about the Flow the way Bastion children read about the sun: a true thing, attested by witnesses, that they will never see.

9) Human Collective

The human nation of the ship, organized as a Collective — commons, living quarters in ordered ranks, an armory, meeting rooms, the Plaza of Humanity and a Grand Hall. Bureaucracy, ceremony, ambition, and dinner, carried on with undiminished seriousness one world away from any ground. The Collective holds its battery (39a) with pride, raids and is raided, intermarries with every tower it fights, and produces — reliably, generation on generation — the ship's clerks, stewards, and chroniclers: if humanity could not rule the great ship, it would at least keep her paperwork.

10) Chalice Tower

A tower named for a cup — and in a city of war-towers, the name is a thesis. The Chalice is one of her old sacred addresses: a tower of ceremony and truce, where factions that cannot meet anywhere else meet under the sign of the poured cup, and truces sworn here are among the few even raiding parties speak of carefully afterward. Its halls host the weddings, funerals, and hostage-exchanges that keep the continual war a society — and in the APOC age it is where envoys from below are received, under a roof where the law of the cup protects even the sphere's strangers.

11) Tower of Thought

The illithid address — named, with the mind flayers' characteristic refusal of euphemism, for the thing they eat. A cathedral to the enclave's garrison citadel (16): its scholars study the ship the way a theologian studies a silent god, and their psionic soundings return, year after year, the same result the gods give the priests — silence, shaped like an answer withheld. The illithids find this delicious. It is the only tower aboard where the mystery is the point.

12) Tower of Trade

Commerce's own fortress — the plan itself marries this tower to the human and halfling towers' armory and storage keys, which is the ship in miniature: trade and war sharing a wall. The Tower fixes the measures aboard (a Spelljammer pound, a Spelljammer bushel, honored from the Gardens to the neogi decks), arbitrates the Market's disputes before they graduate to raids, and maintains the ship's credit — tower-notes carried down to the Rock and back at a discount that tells you, to the fraction, how the season's wars are going. In the steam age it runs on tally engines and pneumatic post.

13) Guild Tower

The makers' tower — seat of her craft guilds: smiths and shipwrights, boiler-keepers and sail-wrights, gardeners' and gunners' fellowships, each with its hall, its marks, its jealously graded mysteries. If the Council is where the ship is almost governed and the Market where she is fed, the Guild Tower is where she is maintained — the highest practical art aboard a living city that can never land or dry-dock. The steam age is, in large part, their age, and a guild master of the Spelljammer can sign his mark on a boiler on the Rock of Bral and be paid double for the signature.

14) Halfling Community

Drawn into the same tower cluster as the Collective — family quarters in warm, low, burrow-scaled floors, and a mess area that is plainly the true capital of the district. The Community is the ship's hearth: cooks, brewers, growers, keepers of the feast days even the war respects — a raid has never yet been launched, the chroniclers note, on a halfling baking day. Too useful to fight, too well-connected by marriage and godparenthood to isolate, the Community endures — and caters the peace conferences.

15) Shivak Terminal

The strangest address in the plan, offered without a word of explanation: a terminal, named for the Shivak, in the heart of the dorsal city — where the ship's internal journeys end: arrivals from the deep decks surface here, departures for the hollow hull go down. Nobody's tower, everybody's crossroads, its notice-boards carry a thousand years of layered paper. In the steam age it has become a station in the full railway sense — steam lifts, counterweight cars, pneumatic post — for her citizens speak of traveling to the far wing the way groundlings speak of the next county, and pack accordingly.

16) Illithid Tower

The stronghold form of the mind flayer presence — the poster's mind flayer outpost at war footing: garrison halls, pools and vaults, and the cold, efficient architecture of a people who regard every other nation aboard as, at best, a long-term resource. The other factions raid it rarely and regret it reliably. Yet it endures within the ship's society: it trades, it treats, it sends its eerie envoys to the Council — because aboard her, even the monsters are citizens.

17) Goblin Alliance Quarters

Pause on the word Alliance — it is the goblinoids' whole achievement aboard: tribes, clans, hobgoblin companies, and bugbear kindreds who arrived as menials, mercenaries, prisoners, and pirate crews, and did the one thing no invader ever managed — they organized. A warren district of dense, perpetually re-fortified housing and workshops turning out the ship's cheap and excellent ironmongery; their chiefs sit in Council by right of a century of being impossible to evict. In the steam age the Alliance has taken to the boiler levels like a birthright — goblin-kept, ship-kept began as an insult and is now a grade mark.

18) Building of the Giff

Not a tower — a Building: broad, parade-square-fronted, immaculate, the headquarters entire of the giff, for whom soldiering is not a trade but a natural state. Barracks kept to a standard that shames palaces, a powder magazine run with religious precision, tailoring establishments of legendary exactness. The giff serve as the ship's professional core — battery officers, marine captains, masters of the Landing Deck's heavy work — the closest thing aboard to a navy. Steam is, to a giff, simply powder that behaves, and a giff bombardier of the Spelljammer is the finest gunner in the sphere.

19) Communal Church

One church, communal — shared — the ship's deliberate answer to what a hundred peoples do about heaven when locked in a hull together forever: a rotating college of priests, a calendar that is a palimpsest of festivals, an architecture every tradition has added to. In the APOC age it is the most philosophically interesting room in the sphere — every priest aboard preaches daily in the condition of gods who can see in and cannot touch, on the one ship the gods would never discuss even before the wall existed. The heavens were always quieter than advertised; worship is what we do for each other meanwhile.

20) Old Wizard's Lair

The plan marks it with the respect due to a hazard: a lair, the word used for a dragon. A wizard of the first rank took up residence aboard in her long past, and the lair has outlasted its maker's tenancy — wards that still turn corridors aside, studies sealed mid-sentence, ten generations of apprentices' graffiti, none of whom ever claimed to have met the Old Wizard in person. The steam age has added a school of thought among her engineers: that the lair is not a residence but a machine — one of the ship's working mysteries, still on duty.

21) Gnoll Ruins

Ruins — the plan's own word, the first it spends on the dead districts. A gnoll nation held a tower here once; what ended them the ship's memory will not settle — plague, a coalition, a civil frenzy, a door opened in the deep decks that should not have been opened. The district stands as they left it, held since by no one, a haunted margin where the continual war itself goes quieter. It is the ship's memento mori, walked past daily by nations at the height of their power: the city is bigger than any nation's ending. The city sails on.

22) Minotaur Tower

Built — who could doubt it — as a labyrinth: a deliberate maze of courts and turns, defensible by a people who navigate it by scent, memory, and a geometry of the blood no invader has ever mapped. Maps of the tower exist in the Library. They are all different; the minotaurs regard this as a successful outcome. The district supplies the ship's most feared boarding troops and its most meticulous warehouse-keepers — the same memory that holds a labyrinth holds an inventory to the last nail.

23) Ogre Wizard Quarters

A district that refutes, by existing, every lazy thing the worlds say about ogres: the quarters of the ogre wizards, a magocracy of giants — spell-houses and observatories, alchemical works scaled to hands like shovels and apparatus built at a size the Guild Tower's examiners handle with long tools and short visits. Transmuters and abjurers of genuine eminence, consulted by the Council on the ship's great workings — and in the steam age, her foremost theorists of scale: what apparatus does when you build it for a hull three-quarters of a mile across.

24) Beholder Ruins

The second of the plan's ruins, and the heavier with meaning. The poster records at least three beholder nations currently aboard — and here is the proof there were once more: a beholder nation's dead district, eye-tyrant architecture gone silent, great spherical chambers and vertical shafts from a people for whom up and down were courtesies. The ruins are worked by licensed wreckers under heavy escort and heavier superstition, and what comes out — beholder optics, levitation stone, the weaponry of the eye-tiers — is among the most valuable salvage in her markets.

25) Dwarven Citadel of Kova

A named citadel — Kova — the only district carrying a proper name like a banner: the dwarven stronghold entire, a fortress-city within the city-ship, engineered like a held breath. Kova is the ship's anvil: its forges feed the engine levels, its Forge-engines are maintained as both machinery and sacrament, its masons hold the Guild warrants for the ship's skeleton, and its thanes sit in Council knowing that when everything aboard has finished failing, the dwarves will be asked to fix it — at their price, in their time. The steam age began here before it began anywhere aboard: Kova never trusted a working it couldn't wrench.

26) Free Dwarves Tower

Kova's necessary counterweight, set beside it in the plan's deliberate geography: the dwarves who are not of Kova — clanless, contract-free, individualists, exiles, republicans, and heretics of the forge. Where the Citadel is a state, the Free Tower is a scene: workshops, drinking halls, radical pamphlets printed on a steam press the Citadel has thrice tried to buy and once tried to burn. Their feud is the ship's oldest continuous argument, conducted almost entirely in masterpieces — rival bridges, rival boilers, rival gates — so the dorsal city's finest engineering is, structurally, an insult exchanged across a street for three hundred years.

27) Neogi Tower

The spider-eel slavers of space, given a stronghold as matter-of-factly as the church — because aboard her everyone is given a stronghold; that is the horror and the genius of the place. A web of a district: vertical, silken, disquieting, its markets dealing in everything the Ship's Stores will not list, umber hulk guards looming in its gateways. Its spymasters are credited with knowing the state of every faction's helm-search to within a corridor — and selling that knowledge, in pieces, to all of them.

28) Hulk Tower

The umber hulks' own address — the ship's parable. The poster records a small herd who escaped from the neogi; the plan records what escaped slaves built when a city-ship gave them ground: a free district of the great burrowing brutes, kept ever since by their descendants and every escaped hulk who has dug his way to it. The hulks work the deep decks and engine levels as sappers — no people alive dig like them — and their tower's law is one sentence long, honored by factions who honor nothing: no chains past this door.

29) Giant Tower

Built at the giants' own measure — doorways like gates, stairs like terraces, furniture like architecture — a district that makes the rest of the city-ship feel briefly like a model of itself. The giants aboard are few and old: a clan-remnant and its adopted kindreds, courted by every faction as allies and by the engine levels as the only workforce that can move certain machinery by hand. Their tower's court keeps the ship's most formal etiquette and its longest memories: a giant here may personally remember a sighting of the ship, from the outside, in a sphere that no longer exists.

30) Shou Tower

The district of the Shou, the spacefaring merchants and mariners of the eastern stars: tea houses and counting houses, astronomical bureaus keeping the ship's most precise star-ledgers, silk and porcelain and lacquer from its workshops, the dragon-prowed silhouette of its ceremonies over the dorsal city on feast days. The Shou took the Closing as a problem in navigation — the sphere is a harbor; harbors are for refitting — and their bureau's sealed-sphere charts, the whole jar sounded and laned, are the finest cartography of the closed world in existence.

31) Arcane's Tower

The Arcane keep a tower, as they keep a presence everywhere ships and helms are dealt in — and of all the addresses in the plan, this is the one the chronicles describe with the most care and the least information. The helm-dealers of the universe maintain it in perfect, courteous, impenetrable order; their envoys sit in Council and say exactly what is needed and not one word more. It is noted in the Library's private marginalia that the Arcane are the only people aboard who have never, in any record, claimed to be searching for the ultimate helm — and that, aboard the Spelljammer, is the loudest possible statement of intent.

32) Teeth Pit

The plan names it with a sailor's delicacy: the ship's arena and abattoir and court of last resort — a sunken ground where the continual war is fought formally, champion against champion, grievance against grievance, before the assembled city, under laws even the beholders observe. Duels, trials by combat, the great beast-fights of the festival calendar. A war that can be scheduled is a war that can, on the other days, be postponed for trade. In the steam age the Pit has acquired steam-raised barriers, pressure-lamp floods, and a tally engine for the betting that is the ship's second economy. The sand is raked. The sand is always raked. Don't ask about the sand.

33) Long Fangs' Tower

The tower of the Long Fangs — the ship's wolf-banner nation, a lycanthrope kindred holding a stronghold in their own right: a den-palace of feasting halls and kennels and trophies, their young fostered across the ship in a web of obligation that makes them quietly one of the best-informed peoples aboard. The Fangs do not want the helm to rule the ship; they want it as the right of the strongest, proved and held and howled over. The moon question — a ship with no world's moon — they answer with their own calendar, kept with astronomical precision: the pack makes its own moons, by law and by blood.

34) Academy of Human Knowledge

The humans' university — where the Collective's clerks, the Council's jurists, and half the ship's engineers begin their letters. Its scholars feud with the Old Elvish Academy (35) in a scholastic war of commentaries unbroken for longer than some towers have stood — the one war aboard fought entirely in footnotes, and therefore the one with no armistice provision. In the APOC age it has taken on the sealed sphere as its great subject: its chairs of Closed Cosmology, of Demonology-under-Seal, of the Economics of the Jar are the finest in the universe that remains.

35) Old Elvish Academy

Old, says the plan — older than the human Academy it feuds with, older, its masters let it be understood, than most things: a school of high wizardry, star-lore, and the long histories, in a tower of such slender and deliberate beauty that the engine districts send their apprentices to look at it once, as a lesson in what precision is for. Its curriculum runs in elvish time, and its silence on the ultimate helm is the second-loudest aboard, after the Arcane's. In the steam age it has admitted apparatus into its curriculum as a classical discipline — as if steam were a dead language it had graciously decided to revive.

36) Elven High Command

The sword to the Academy's word: headquarters of the elven nation aboard, heirs of the Man-o-War fleets and the Armada tradition — spelljamming's first navy, never stopped being a fleet even beached on the back of a greater ship. Chart rooms lit like chapels, signal masts, the austere messes of officers whose service is measured in centuries. When the factions unite against a boarder, an elven staff draws the plan, by custom no one now contests. Their helm-search is the most poignant aboard: they seek it not to use it but to garrison it — and they alone would find it and, in perfect discipline, tell no one.

37) Armory

The plan gives the ship one district named simply the Armory — and every reader aboard understands this is the known one: the public arsenal, distinct in every mind from the great armories deep within the hull of the poster's stories, whose guardians have never corrected the confusion helpfully. A fortress-warehouse of record: catapult and ballista parks under steam winches, shot and stone and iron in graded mountains, issued to the batteries (39a–d) under warrant and audited by a staff of incorruptible and therefore much-assassinated auditors. In the steam age it has become a factory as well as a vault.

38) Dark Tower

The plan does not explain this one. It doesn't have to. What nation holds it, the chronicles rotate: a death cult, a vampire's court, a brotherhood of assassins, a college of shadow-mages, a single tenant whose name the Library files under seal. What is constant is its function: the place where the things the Council cannot do, the Market cannot price, and the Church cannot bless are nonetheless done — under a roof, by appointment, at professional rates. In the APOC age it is where the ship's darkest expertise is kept, the way a city keeps its plague hospital: walled, watched, and profoundly unabolished.

39a) Battery — Human Contingent

The human nation's gun-deck: one of the four great faction batteries, crewed by the Collective's gunners under their own officers. A battery aboard her is not a weapon; it is a parish — guns with names, crews with dynasties, a roll of the honored dead painted where the powder passes. In the steam refit the human battery took the traversing gear and winch-work early and completely — the Tower of Trade financed it, the Guild warranted it — and its broadside drill on festival days, blank shot over the dark wing, is the dorsal city's favorite thunder.

39b) Battery — Dwarven Contingent

Kova's guns: the heaviest pieces, the deepest magazines, mountings built like the Citadel itself — and, since the steam refit, the proving ground of the whole broadside, for the traversing engines and counterweight rams that now serve every battery were dwarven work first, tested under dwarven standards: conditions intended to break them, in front of witnesses, with the failures published. A dwarven battery crew does not hurry. A dwarven battery crew does not miss, either — the two facts are the same fact.

39c) Battery — Elvish Contingent

The broadside as ballet: the lightest, longest-ranged pieces, served by crews for whom gunnery is a branch of astronomy — every shot a computation in star-angle and drift, laid by officers who learned the mathematics in the Old Academy and the manners in the High Command. Where the dwarven battery is a hammer and the human battery a volley, the elvish battery is a sentence, delivered once, precisely, at a range the other contingents discuss like weather in another country. Its guns are the ship's most beautiful — living-wood and bright metal in the Man-o-War tradition.

39d) Battery — Neogi Contingent

The fourth battery is the neogi's — the ship's whole philosophy mounted in iron: the slavers of space serve the great ship's broadside as a contingent of her defense, under their own officers, from their own arc, because aboard the Spelljammer the alternative to arming your monsters is being boarded by somebody else's. The neogi battery is, by every account including its rivals', superb — umber hulk crews of terrifying strength serving spider-drilled gun teams. What the contingent is paid, beyond its keep and its arc, is the ship's most negotiated secret.

40) Dracon Tower

The dracons' tower — the great centaur-dragons of space, largest of her peoples in body and among the gravest in council, holding a district built at their full scale: halls like hangars, a tower half stronghold and half stable-palace. The dracons serve as her heavy couriers and living siege-engines, and their word in Council carries the weight of a people nobody has accused of frivolity. Their helm-search is the ship's most philosophical: the dracons hold, as tower doctrine, that the ship is not steered at all — that the ultimate helm is a story the smaller peoples tell about a freedom too large to look at directly.

41) Centaur Tower

The last number in the plan, and the plan ends the city as it should be ended: at a gallop. Archer-clans and far-ranging couriers, a tower built around courts and paddocks and a great running-ground where the wing's edge lets a herd thunder along three-quarters of a mile of open deck under open stars — the single most joyful sight the ship offers. The centaurs are her messengers and her memory of distance: in a city that is also a vessel, where every other people walks, they run. Their helm-doctrine, sung rather than written, is the one the whole city secretly believes — the wise do not seek to steer her. The wise seek to keep up.

What the record has not settled

  • Where the dead go, now that the planes are cut.
  • The outcomes and extent of the demon incursions on the other worlds, world by world.

The Spelljammer layer is compiled from the campaign's own incorporated records: the boxed set (Shaun's copy of Spelljammer: Adventures in Space), the wider 2e line gathered into the game (Lost Ships, War Captain's Companion, The Legend of Spelljammer, The Astromundi Cluster, and more), Bastion Press Airships, Pirates!, Shaun's own homebrew ships and files, and the deck plans kept in his maps collection. The sheets shown on this page are the collection's own: the Rock of Bral's map with the station's entry, the Spelljammer Academy map with the workings, and — filed with The Spelljammer's own entry in the fleet, as the ship's maps, not the sphere's — her poster map and her forty-one-area deck plans. The Rock of Bral and The Spelljammer expansions ride here in full as ordered — updated and steampunkified, every point elaborated — sitting separate from settled canon until Shaun approves them in.
